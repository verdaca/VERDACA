## ⚠️ §A.0 — TOKONOMICS-ERA ICP FIREWALL BANNER (load-bearing)

> **READ BEFORE USING THIS DOCUMENT.** This banner is load-bearing for every downstream agent (6.0.2 Maya, 6.1 Winston, and every Studio artifact that follows). Do **not** strip, paraphrase, or relocate it.

**Praxis Studio's hypothesized ICP is NOT the tokonomics ICP.** The tokonomics business session (`Tokonomics/business_session.md`, 2026-04-10) captured Voice-of-Customer work for a predecessor product targeting **developer-cost buyers** — AI-native SaaS VPs of Engineering, agent-builder CTOs, and RAG-heavy Directors of Engineering. Tokonomics buyers speak **cost-engineering dialect**: "token bill," "compression ratio," "gateway," "proxy," "per-call cost," "LLM spend," "LangChain callback," "SDK integration," "self-serve free tier."

**Praxis Studio's hypothesized ICP speaks a different language entirely.** The three hypothesized segments — Series A–C founders, mid-market COOs, and boutique strategy consultancies — show up with **strategic-decision dialect**: "bridge round," "runway," "optionality," "acquire vs. build," "steelman," "blind spots," "moat," "red team," "signaling risk," "sequencing," "board is asking."

**The firewall rule:**

1. **No tokonomics-era persona (VP-Eng / agent-builder CTO / RAG Dir-of-Eng) appears in this document, and none should appear in any Stage 6 Studio artifact.** If a downstream artifact imports those personas, it is importing the wrong ICP.
2. **No tokonomics-era vocabulary appears in this document.** Terms like *compression, token, proxy, gateway, SDK, callback handler, LangChain integration, per-call cost, LLM spend, pip install* are **OUT OF SCOPE** for Studio customer language. They belong to a different product targeting a different buyer.
3. **The tokonomics brand voice archetype** ("direct, technical, honest, dry-witted, confident — the engineer two stools over who shows you a terminal screenshot") is a **producer-brand observation**, not a customer-voice observation. It is deliberately **not** propagated into hypothesized Studio customer quotes in §A. Studio's brand voice is a separate Stage 6 question to be answered at 6.0.3, not inherited from tokonomics by default.
4. **The tokonomics-era pricing work** (gain-share 20% of savings, $200/mo floor, tooling-budget bands in the $500–$2K/mo range) is a **tokonomics pricing observation**, not a Studio pricing observation. Studio pricing will be set against a **strategic-advisory budget anchor**, not a **tooling-budget anchor**. Do not import numeric WTP claims from tokonomics into Studio.

**Why this firewall exists:** without it, the natural gravity of the tokonomics corpus — which has more persona detail, more finished GTM language, and higher document completeness than any Studio-native source — would pull downstream Studio artifacts toward the wrong ICP by default. Maya (6.0.2) would build an empathy map for a VP of Engineering who doesn't exist in Studio's world. Winston (6.1) would pick the wrong brand voice archetype. Stage 6.6 pre-sales would present a Studio demo to the wrong buyer shape. The firewall prevents that failure mode at the inheritance boundary.

**If a future document wants to import tokonomics-era language into Studio context, the import must go through an explicit ADR in 6.0.3 (advanced elicitation) naming the specific migration rationale — not silent osmosis.**

## §C — Jargon Glossary

> **⚠️ ALL ENTRIES HYPOTHETICAL — term-level observations about what each segment's dialect probably contains, pending §D interviews. Every entry carries `[HYPOTHETICAL]`.**

### §C.1 — Founder Dialect

- **bridge round** — a financing round raised between primary rounds, usually at flat or discounted terms, to extend runway into better market conditions. Founder-native. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **flat round** — a primary round priced at the previous round's valuation. Signals investor caution without full down-round stigma. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **runway** — months of operation remaining at current burn rate. Temporal and strategic unit for founders. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **burn** — monthly net cash outflow. Always positive; "cutting burn" = reducing cash outflow. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **optionality** — the set of future strategic moves preserved or destroyed by a current decision. Founders defend optionality; lock-in destroys it. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **lock-in** — commitment that forecloses future options. Typically used pejoratively ("three-year lock-in," "vendor lock-in"). `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **moat** — defensible competitive advantage that survives pricing pressure and commoditization. Discussed most intensively by founders whose moat is under active threat (Q5, Q8). `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **signal vs. noise** — whether a small number of data points (e.g., 2 EU inbound leads) constitutes evidence of real demand or statistical noise. Q7 framing. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **sequencing risk** — risk introduced by the order in which actions are taken, independent of the actions themselves. Q1 transition-window vocabulary. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **migration period** — the window during which old and new states coexist. Q1 pricing transition, Q9 GTM pivot. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **at-risk account** — a customer whose retention is uncertain, usually due to pricing pressure, product dissatisfaction, or champion turnover. Q6, Q10. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **reference customer** — a customer whose public name and logo carries disproportionate signaling weight for new sales. Losing one has signaling cost beyond the MRR loss. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **hybrid trap** — a compromise approach (e.g., "do SMB and enterprise simultaneously") that fails at both ends. Q9. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **acqui-hire** — acquisition where the primary value is the team, not the technology or revenue. Q3 sub-variant. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **time-to-market** — calendar time between decision and in-market availability. Often destroyed by integration reality after acquisition. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **ninety-day horizon** — the operating-decision horizon founders plan against (vs. the yearly horizon of strategy). `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **steelman** — partial founder penetration. Higher among founders who have formal strategy consulting backgrounds; lower among first-time founders. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`

### §C.2 — Mid-Market COO Dialect

- **decision architecture** — the structure by which a decision will be made: factors, weights, sequence, tie-breakers. COOs buy decision architecture, not recommendations. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **integration reality** — the actual time, cost, and failure rate of post-acquisition integration, typically diverging from acquisition-model assumptions. Q3-native. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **loaded cost** — the fully-burdened cost of engineering or operational capacity including benefits, overhead, and opportunity cost. Used in Q3 build-vs-buy framing. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **capability gap specificity** — the requirement that a capability-gap analysis name the specific missing capability, not a category (e.g., "real-time fraud signal enrichment," not "fraud capabilities"). `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **operational readiness** — a pre-execution checklist of non-technical conditions (team, process, measurement, escalation) that determine whether an initiative can execute. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **change management** — the discipline of moving organizational state between two configurations without breaking existing workflows. COO-favored framing. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **diagnostic plan** — a structured investigation protocol with specified analyses, timelines, and decision points. Q10 output shape. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **composition effect** — aggregate metric movement caused by shifting underlying mix, not by changes in unit-level behavior. Q10 specific. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`
- **sponsor coverage** — the set of internal stakeholders whose buy-in is required for an initiative to survive organizational friction. `[HYPOTHETICAL]`

### §D.1 — Series A–C Founder Interview Guide (~15 questions, primary segment)

**Phase 1 — Context and Role (2 questions)**

- D.1.1: "What's the company at today — stage, team size, what you're building?" [Expected signal: opens with stage + product + team; stumbles into ARR/growth metrics only if prompted. *Falsification:* if respondent frames primarily in terms of technology stack or engineering metrics rather than market position, they are likely closer to the tokonomics VP-Eng persona than the Studio founder persona — reclassify.]
- D.1.2: "How would you describe your role in strategic decisions vs. operational ones?" [Expected signal: founder-realism about both — "I make the call on strategy and I'm in the weeds on operations more than I'd like." *Falsification:* if respondent cleanly separates strategic from operational and claims they only work on strategic, reclassify toward COO or board-operator.]

**Phase 2 — Recent Strategic Decision Pain (4 questions, concrete only)**

- D.1.3: "Walk me through the hardest strategic decision you've had to make in the last ninety days. Not hypothetical — a real one." [Expected signal: runway/pricing/partnership/hire-fire/pivot; operator-realism register. *Falsification:* if no real decision surfaces, the founder may be pre-product-market-fit and out of the hypothesized Studio ICP.]
- D.1.4: "What made it hard — what was the part you couldn't get clean on?" [Expected signal: uncertainty language around specific sub-questions; conflicting inputs from different advisors; missing data. This is the Studio opportunity surface. *Falsification:* if the blocker is purely informational (e.g., "I needed one specific data point and couldn't get it"), Studio may not be the right tool for that decision.]
- D.1.5: "Who did you talk to about it — formally or informally?" [Expected signal: named sources (CFO, board member, a specific advisor, a specific peer, an investor). Count of distinct sources = quality of decision-support ecosystem. *Falsification:* if the respondent talked only to themselves or to one person, there is no "conflicting advice" pain for Studio to address.]
- D.1.6: "What did the different people tell you, and did they agree?" [Expected signal: explicit disagreement narrated, often across functional lines (finance vs. product vs. sales). This is the single most load-bearing question in the guide — the pain Studio is hypothesized to address exists only if this question produces real conflict. *Falsification:* if all advisors agreed, the "conflicting advice" pain hypothesis collapses for this founder.]

**Phase 3 — Current Advisor Landscape (3 questions)**

- D.1.7: "When you need a second opinion on a strategic question, what do you actually do today — concretely, in the last time it happened?" [Expected signal: a mixed bag of board members, ex-operator peers, McKinsey-adjacent alumni friends, paid fractional advisors, and increasingly AI chatbots. *Falsification:* if the respondent goes directly to a single trusted advisor and gets an answer that ends the process, there is no "structured decision support" pain.]
- D.1.8: "Have you used AI chatbots for strategic questions? If so, when did it work and when did it fall short?" [Expected signal: mixed results, often with specific failure modes — "it told me what I already knew," "it hedged too much," "it didn't push back," "it gave me one answer when I needed to see the trade-offs." *Falsification:* if the respondent has zero experience with AI for strategic questions and refuses to consider it, the Studio positioning faces adoption resistance.]
- D.1.9: "What would a good version of a 'strategic second opinion' tool look like to you?" [Expected signal: explicit features the founder values — usually structured trade-offs, explicit dissent, named scenarios, invalidation conditions. Match these against the MAC quality-rubric gates (without naming them). *Falsification:* if the founder describes a tool that is explicitly single-answer and non-dissenting, Studio's multi-perspective shape is misaligned with this buyer.]

**Phase 4 — "What We're Not Seeing" Probing (3 questions)**

- D.1.10: "Can you think of a strategic decision in the last year where the thing that mattered most was something you only noticed later?" [Expected signal: a blind-spot story — "the integration was harder than we modeled," "the partner got acquired," "the team retention was the real constraint." *Falsification:* if the respondent cannot surface any blind-spot story, either they have unusually good decision hygiene or they lack the self-reflection needed to become a Studio buyer.]
- D.1.11: "When you make a big strategic call, how do you check for blind spots — do you have a protocol, or is it 'talk to smart people and hope for the best'?" [Expected signal: most founders will admit to the informal version. Some will describe formal red-teaming rituals. *Falsification:* N/A — this question surfaces protocol shape.]
- D.1.12: "If a tool could tell you 'here are the three risks you haven't considered in this decision,' how much would that be worth to you in time, not dollars?" [Expected signal: time-units — "a week of worry," "a board meeting," "a consulting engagement avoided." **Do not ask for dollar WTP — that is forbidden by Constraint 3.** *Falsification:* if the respondent dismisses the value proposition entirely, reclassify.]

**Phase 5 — Category Reaction (3 questions, abstract only, no product demo)**

- D.1.13: "Suppose there was a service that took a strategic question, ran it through a structured analysis with multiple perspectives — including a steelman of the opposite position and a list of what the analysis didn't cover — and produced a document you could put in front of your board. No demo, no product. Just the shape of that. Does that resonate?" [Expected signal: cautious interest with specific concerns ("how long does it take," "what does it cost in time not money," "can I trust the output"). *Falsification:* flat disinterest means the hypothesized pain doesn't exist for this founder; enthusiastic agreement without concerns may indicate politeness rather than real interest.]
- D.1.14: "What would make you skeptical of that kind of output?" [Expected signal: specific failure modes — hallucination, generic answers, confident-but-wrong, too long, too short, wrong frame, missed the real question. These map directly to the MAC quality-rubric gates and should not be named back. *Falsification:* N/A — surfaces objections.]
- D.1.15: "If the output was good, what would you do with it — put it in the board deck, use it to argue with the CFO, keep it in your own notes, or something else?" [Expected signal: deliverable-shape preference — board-ready document, conversation-enabler artifact, private thinking aid. Drives §6.0.3 output format ADRs. *Falsification:* if respondent wouldn't use the output for anything, value hypothesis collapses.]

### §D.2 — Mid-Market COO Interview Guide (~15 questions, secondary segment)

**Phase 1 — Context and Role (2 questions)**

- D.2.1: "What's the company at today, and where does your role sit in the strategic-vs-operational split?" [Expected signal: COO realism — "I own execution, the CEO owns strategy, but strategic calls with big operational implications come through me." *Falsification:* if respondent describes role purely in back-office terms (HR, legal, facilities), they may be a COO in title only and closer to a Chief Administrative Officer — reclassify.]
- D.2.2: "When the CEO is making a big strategic call, what's your role in the decision architecture — sounding board, veto, execution planner, something else?" [Expected signal: "decision architecture" language should feel native to the respondent. *Falsification:* if the term "decision architecture" feels foreign and the respondent substitutes "decision-making," reclassify toward operational director.]

**Phase 2 — Recent Decision Architecture Pain (4 questions)**

- D.2.3: "Walk me through the most recent strategic decision where you were asked to help structure the decision itself — the factors, the weighting, the sequence — rather than asked to give an opinion on the outcome." [Expected signal: acquire vs. build, pivot vs. double-down, reorganization, capability build vs. buy. *Falsification:* if the respondent cannot distinguish "structure the decision" from "give an opinion," reclassify.]
- D.2.4: "When you structure a decision like that, where do you typically start — with the factors, with the options, with the constraints, or with the stakeholders?" [Expected signal: most experienced COOs start with constraints or stakeholders, not factors. *Falsification:* N/A — surfaces methodological style.]
- D.2.5: "What part of structuring that decision was hardest — was it finding the factors, weighting them, or getting alignment on the weighting?" [Expected signal: alignment is typically the hardest; factor-finding is second; weighting is first-order technical work. *Falsification:* if the respondent says "none of it was hard," they may not be making decisions at the COO altitude the hypothesized ICP assumes.]
- D.2.6: "Did the decision go through a structured process, or was it mostly in conversations?" [Expected signal: mostly conversations, with structured artifacts (decks, memos, RACI charts) appearing late in the process. *Falsification:* if a fully-structured process is described end-to-end, the COO may already have a solution in place.]

**Phase 3 — Integration Reality and Implementation Risk (3 questions)**

- D.2.7: "When you think about a big strategic call — acquire a company, launch a new product line, enter a new market — what's the part of the analysis that's usually wrong in hindsight?" [Expected signal: integration reality, team retention during change, timeline slippage, hidden second-order costs. *Falsification:* if the respondent says "the analysis is usually right," either they have superhuman decision hygiene or unusually low decision stakes.]
- D.2.8: "How do you check for what's missing from a decision memo before you approve it?" [Expected signal: some combination of asking specific team members, running past counterexamples, checking against a mental checklist of failure modes from prior experience. *Falsification:* N/A — surfaces checking discipline.]
- D.2.9: "If a tool could highlight what a strategic decision memo is NOT addressing — the factors it's silent on — how much would that matter to you?" [Expected signal: COOs should be more receptive to this than founders because scope-naming is a COO-native discipline. *Falsification:* flat disinterest suggests the COO either doesn't own strategy-execution bridging or already has a mental model for this problem.]

**Phase 4 — Diagnostic Protocol Probing (3 questions, Q10-anchored)**

- D.2.10: "Have you ever had a situation where two metrics told contradictory stories and you had to figure out which one was right?" [Expected signal: a specific diagnostic story — NPS vs. retention, growth vs. churn, CAC vs. LTV, engagement vs. revenue. *Falsification:* if the respondent hasn't experienced diverging metrics, either their metric hygiene is low or their company is early-stage enough that metric divergence hasn't surfaced yet.]
- D.2.11: "How did you go about figuring out which one was right — what was your diagnostic approach?" [Expected signal: generating competing hypotheses, designing analyses to distinguish between them, assigning specific analyses to specific people with specific deadlines. *Falsification:* if the respondent describes "we just looked at the data harder," the diagnostic discipline isn't there.]
- D.2.12: "What would a good diagnostic plan look like to you — structurally?" [Expected signal: hypotheses + specific analyses + decision points + timelines. This maps directly to Q10 gold-standard but should not be named back. *Falsification:* N/A — surfaces decision-architecture style.]

**Phase 5 — Category Reaction (3 questions, abstract only)**

- D.2.13: "Suppose there was a service that took a complex decision — say, acquire vs. build, or a metric-divergence diagnostic — and produced a structured decision memo with the factors laid out, the trade-offs explicit, the dissent represented, and an explicit list of what wasn't analyzed. Does that resonate?" [Expected signal: COO interest should be higher than founder interest because decision-architecture shape is COO-native. *Falsification:* flat disinterest means the hypothesis collapses.]
- D.2.14: "What would make you trust the output of a tool like that?" [Expected signal: traceability (where did each claim come from), methodological transparency (how was the analysis run), named failure modes (what the tool explicitly doesn't do), and some form of provenance trail. *Falsification:* N/A — surfaces trust architecture.]
- D.2.15: "Who would you share the output with — the CEO, the exec team, the board, your own team, or only yourself?" [Expected signal: deliverable-routing preference by decision type. Drives §6.0.3 output format ADRs. *Falsification:* if the respondent wouldn't share the output, the social-artifact dimension collapses.]

### §D.3 — Boutique Strategy Consultancy Interview Guide (~15 questions, tertiary segment)

> **Structural split:** Phase 1–3 focus on *engagement economics* (what boutique strategy consultancies currently deliver and at what unit economics). Phase 4–5 focus on *Praxis as a leverage tool* (would a consultant use Praxis to produce their own deliverables faster, or to resell Praxis output directly). The difference between these two answers determines the channel thesis. **Fractional C-suite operators are a Stage-7 deferred expansion segment** — not interviewed in 6.0.1; at Stage 7, extend this guide with a fractional-C-suite variant.

**Phase 1 — Firm and Engagement Context (2 questions)**

- D.3.1: "Tell me about the firm — how many partners, how many analysts, typical engagement size, typical engagement length." [Expected signal: 1–5 partners, 0–10 analysts, engagements in the $50K–$250K range over 4–12 weeks. *Falsification:* if larger (50+ consultants, $1M+ engagements), reclassify toward mid-size strategy firm; if smaller (solo operator only, <$50K), reclassify toward fractional advisory.]
- D.3.2: "What fraction of your engagements are strategy-framing work vs. research vs. implementation support?" [Expected signal: strategy-framing should be dominant for the boutique-strategy category. *Falsification:* if primarily research or implementation, reclassify toward research shop or implementation consultancy.]

**Phase 2 — Current Delivery Shape (3 questions)**

- D.3.3: "Walk me through how a typical strategic decision engagement runs — what gets delivered at week one, week four, end of engagement?" [Expected signal: scoping week one, workshop + interviews weeks two-three, structured analysis week four, decision memo / board deck end of engagement. *Falsification:* highly unstructured delivery suggests mis-categorization.]
- D.3.4: "What's the most analyst-hour-intensive part of the delivery?" [Expected signal: factor identification, alternative generation, scenario modeling, stakeholder interviews synthesis. The answer names the leverage opportunity. *Falsification:* N/A — surfaces leverage target.]
- D.3.5: "What does a 'dense enough' deliverable look like versus a 'thin' deliverable — what makes the difference?" [Expected signal: explicit dissent, named alternatives, specific invalidation conditions, traceable logic, not just conclusions. *Falsification:* if the respondent can't articulate the quality difference, engagement leverage may not be their primary concern.]

**Phase 3 — Engagement Leverage and Tool Adoption (3 questions)**

- D.3.6: "What tools have you adopted in the last two years that changed your engagement economics?" [Expected signal: probably research-augmentation tools (Perplexity, ChatGPT-for-research), deck tools, data tools. *Falsification:* if zero tools adopted, the firm may be tool-averse and not a viable channel.]
- D.3.7: "When you evaluate a new tool, is the decision made at the partner level, the firm level, or individually by analysts?" [Expected signal: varies — mature firms decide at firm level; younger firms decide at partner level. Drives go-to-market motion. *Falsification:* N/A — surfaces decision authority.]
- D.3.8: "What would a tool need to do to reduce your hours-per-deliverable by a meaningful amount without reducing deliverable density?" [Expected signal: automate the structured-analysis phase, preserve the judgment phase. This is the Studio leverage hypothesis. *Falsification:* if the respondent says "nothing — our value is in the judgment, automation doesn't help," the leverage thesis collapses for this firm.]

**Phase 4 — Praxis as Leverage Tool (3 questions)**

- D.3.9: "Suppose there was a tool that took a strategic question — say, acquire vs. build — and produced a structured analysis with factors, trade-offs, dissent, and an explicit list of what wasn't covered. How would you think about using that in an engagement?" [Expected signal: two patterns to distinguish — (a) "use it to accelerate our own analysis, reframe in our voice, bill as our work" = leverage-tool thesis, or (b) "resell the output directly to clients as a white-label deliverable" = channel thesis. **These are fundamentally different channel strategies and the answer determines Praxis's consultancy go-to-market.** *Falsification:* if neither pattern emerges, the consultancy channel hypothesis collapses for this firm.]
- D.3.10: "Would you want to present the output of a tool like that as your work, or would you want to cite the tool?" [Expected signal: almost always 'our work' — client-white-labelability is near-universal in boutique strategy. *Falsification:* if the respondent insists on citing the tool, they may be atypically methodology-transparent or they may be signaling discomfort with the leverage-tool pattern.]
- D.3.11: "How much would it matter to you that the tool's output is structured the same way a strategy deliverable is structured — findings, dissent, alternatives, scope limits — vs. looking like a chatbot response?" [Expected signal: it should matter a lot. Consultancies are brand-sensitive about output shape. *Falsification:* if it doesn't matter, the firm may not be in the boutique-strategy category.]

**Phase 5 — Category Reaction and Channel Thesis (3 questions)**

- D.3.12: "If you were recommending a tool like this to your partners, what would you want to be true about it before you'd put your reputation behind it?" [Expected signal: traceability, quality consistency, confidentiality of client inputs, no surprise updates, no embarrassing failure modes. *Falsification:* if the respondent wouldn't recommend it at all, the firm-level adoption path is dead and only individual-partner adoption remains viable.]
- D.3.13: "How would you think about pricing this tool to your firm — per seat, per engagement, per deliverable, or something else?" [Expected signal: pricing-structure preference observation. **Do not ask for a dollar number — that is forbidden by Constraint 3.** The relevant signal is the STRUCTURE, not the NUMBER. *Falsification:* N/A — surfaces commercial-structure preference.]
- D.3.14: "Is there a version of this that you would actively NOT want to exist — something about the tool shape that would bother you or your partners?" [Expected signal: the leverage-tool anxiety — "it makes analysts less skilled," "it lets unqualified firms compete with us," "it commoditizes what we sell." *Falsification:* if zero concerns, the respondent is either enthusiastic or disengaged; probe for which.]
- D.3.15: "Who else at the firm should I talk to about a tool like this — and who would be the blocker?" [Expected signal: named individuals, often split between adopter and skeptic. Drives the firm-level go-to-market. *Falsification:* N/A — surfaces firm-level decision topology.]
